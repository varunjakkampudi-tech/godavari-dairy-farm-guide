Recognize danger and call a veterinarian

This is an education and escalation guide, not a diagnosis. Buffalo illness can worsen quickly. Call a veterinarian for persistent appetite loss, fever, sudden milk drop, abnormal milk, lameness, diarrhea, injury or reproductive problems. Immediate emergency response is required for breathing distress, inability to rise, severe bloat, suspected poisoning, difficult calving, heavy bleeding or collapse.

Evidence and safety: General guidance only. Confirm plot, buyer, legal and veterinary decisions with current local authorities and qualified professionals. Unverified regional details remain explicitly marked.

These cards are conservative orientation summaries based on the linked official references. They are not diagnosis or treatment protocols, have not been reviewed by a licensed local veterinarian, and are not approved for clinical use. Signs overlap; call a veterinarian and follow district/official disease-control instructions. No doses, injection steps or lay treatment procedures are provided.

Why it matters

Safe first response: keep yourself safe; move the animal to a quiet shaded area if this can be done safely; provide access to clean water if alert and able to drink; separate animals when infectious disease is plausible; note onset, temperature only if trained, appetite, water intake, manure, milk changes, calving/treatment dates; call the vet and follow directions.

Practical guidance

Avoid force-drenching a weak or gasping animal, inserting objects, cutting, untrained injections/IVs, human medicines, leftover antibiotics, unverified remedies, or delaying care while searching online. Do not sell or mix milk from a treated animal until a veterinarian confirms the withdrawal period.

Use this safely

Mastitis signs may include a hot/swollen/painful quarter, clots or watery milk, fever or production drop. Keep abnormal milk separate, use hygienic milking, and seek veterinary examination/testing. Treatment and milk withdrawal depend on veterinary diagnosis and medicine.

Local application

Fever, sudden fall in yield, anorexia, bloat, recumbency, severe diarrhea, respiratory distress, suspected FMD lesions, abortion or retained placenta require prompt professional assessment. Record advice, diagnosis, treatment and withdrawal end date per animal.

Evidence and limits

Signs overlap across many conditions and can be difficult to assess. Buffalo-specific and official sources support sign recognition and outbreak reporting; the urgency routing here is a conservative educational aid and has not been clinically validated by a local veterinarian.

Emergency contacts and what to report

Emergency decision path

Breathing distress, collapse, cannot rise, severe bloat, suspected poison, difficult calving, heavy bleeding? Call a veterinarian or emergency animal service immediately. Keep people safe, avoid force-drenching or untrained interventions, and follow professional instructions.

Sudden milk drop, fever, appetite loss, abnormal milk, diarrhea or lameness? Separate abnormal milk, observe and record onset/signs, call the veterinarian promptly, and do not use leftover medicine.
